# What a strategy note is

Uberschach's opening explorer shows a board and, beside it, a panel. After
each move the panel shows the move with its rating (Best, Good, Playable,
Inaccuracy, Mistake), the name of the opening it belongs to, a short
history (the "background"), and then the **strategy note** of that move.
Below the panel, the next moves worth learning are listed with their names
and drawn as arrows on the board; each of them has its own note, read when
the visitor plays it.

The reader is a club player who knows the rules and the pieces and is
learning openings, not a theory specialist.

## What the note says

The note explains what **this move** means:

- **what it wants**: the idea, the aim, what it fights for;
- **what it gives up or risks**: the cost, the weakness, the commitment;
- **what kind of game it leads to**: open or closed, sharp or quiet, who
  sets the agenda, the long-term trade (space against solidity, a pawn
  structure, king safety, development).

It should be useful and understandable: a reader who never plays on
should still know why the move is played, or why it is not.

## What it avoids

- **Naming concrete follow-up moves as the options to come.** "White then
  picks 3.Bb5, 3.Bc4, 3.d4 or 3.Nc3" or "Black meets it with ...Nf6, ...Bc5
  or ...Be7" is a tour of the next entries; the explorer shows each of them
  as its own entry, with its own note. The same goes for listing the
  openings that can follow ("the Ruy Lopez, the Italian, the Scotch").
- **The same on moves rated 1 to 3.** The book does not go on after them,
  but what the opponent does with the time such a move gives away is still
  said in words, not as the moves that do it: "White builds the centre
  with Nc3, d4 and e4 at leisure" becomes "White builds a full centre at
  leisure"; "Black takes the centre with ...d5 and ...e6" becomes "Black
  takes the centre at once"; "with ...Nf6 and ...d5 to follow; White can
  meet it with Qc2 or a3" goes. The reply that refutes the move or wins
  what it hangs stays: that reply is the move's meaning.
- **Opening labels as shorthand.** "Transposes into a Slav", "Benko
  style", "a Philidor-like set-up", "Old Indian or King's Indian set-ups":
  say what the set-up is (a solid pawn chain, a pawn sacrifice for
  queenside pressure, a compact defence of e5) instead of naming openings
  the reader may not know. The name of the opening the move itself belongs
  to is shown above the note and need not be repeated.
- **Technical language for its own sake.** Use the words a club player
  knows (centre, development, castling, tempo, initiative, fianchetto, pawn
  structure, open file, long diagonal); describe anything rarer in plain
  words instead of naming it (not "hypermodern", but "controls the centre
  from a distance instead of occupying it").
- **History.** Names of players, dates, games and books belong in the
  background, which is sourced separately. Do not repeat the background or
  the name either: they are shown just above.

Move notation is **not** forbidden. It depends on what the sentence states:

- A square is fine when the square is the point ("fights for the d5 square").
- When a concrete reply is the reason a move is weak, name it: that reply
  is the move's meaning, not a forecast ("hangs the bishop: Black simply
  takes it"; "...d5 at once threatens to take the knight and double White's
  pawns"). In words or in notation, whichever is clearer.
- What a move prepares or prevents can be its meaning ("prepares to
  fianchetto the queen's bishop", "keeps Black from occupying the centre
  with the e-pawn"). Say it in words when words are as clear.

The test for each sentence: **is it about what this move does, or a tour
of the moves that come next?** Keep the first, cut the second.

## Form

- Two or three sentences, about 20 to 55 words; a weak move may need only
  one or two.
- Present tense. British spelling (centre, defence, manoeuvre). English
  piece letters in notation (Nf3, Bb5; Black's moves as ...Nf6).
- The notes of one position are read side by side: each says what is
  specific to its move. Ten weak moves that all "do nothing for the centre"
  are noise; say what each one actually does and costs.
- Clarity comes before variety. Never reword a clear sentence only so that
  it reads differently from a sibling; several notes may start with a verb.
- A note may start with the verb, its subject being the move ("Occupies
  the centre and ..."), or with a short characterisation ("The most natural
  reply: ...").
- Say no more than the board and the sources bear: "weakens the kingside"
  is not "leaves the king without pawn cover".
- Every concrete claim about the position (what attacks, defends, blocks
  or hangs what) is true on the board. Check it with the board tool; in
  earlier rounds the most frequent error was a note that was false on the
  board.

## Examples

**A move worth learning, lifted** (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6, rated 5).
Before:

> Defends e5 with a developing move and adds control of d4. White then
> picks the battleground: 3.Bb5 (the Ruy Lopez), 3.Bc4 (the Italian Game),
> 3.d4 (the Scotch Game) or 3.Nc3 (the Three Knights Game). Black meets
> each with natural development, ...Nf6, ...Bc5 or ...Be7 and early
> castling, but has to know them all, since White decides which one arises.

After:

> The most natural reply: Black defends the attacked pawn and develops a
> piece in the same move, and the knight joins the fight for the centre.
> Black commits to nothing yet, and that is both the strength and the cost.
> Every set-up stays open, but White now decides what kind of game it
> becomes, from slow manoeuvring to sharp open play, and Black has to be at
> home in all of them.

**A weak move, trimmed** (1.e4 e5 2.Ke2, rated 1). Before:

> Gives up the right to castle and blocks both the queen and the f1-bishop,
> so the king must move again before White can develop normally. Black
> simply develops with ...Nf6, ...Nc6 and ...d5 and opens the centre
> against the stranded king.

After:

> Gives up castling and blocks both the queen and the king's bishop, so
> the king has to move again before White can develop normally. Black
> needs nothing special: quick development and an open centre are enough
> against the stranded king.

**A weak move, kept** (1.e4 e5 2.Ba6, rated 1). The reply that refutes it
is the whole point, so the note stays:

> Hangs the bishop: 2...bxa6 or 2...Nxa6 wins it for nothing, and White
> has no compensation for the lost piece.
